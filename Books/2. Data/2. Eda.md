## 2.2 Quantization
Quantization is the process of changing the dtype of a model's weights, before, during, or after training. This allows the model to run more smoothly on basic computers

> PyTorch defaults to float32 (spending 32 bits per weight)

| dtype | Bits | Memory by weight | Precision |
|-----------|------|----------------|-------------|
| float32 | 32 | 4 bytes | High |
| float16 | 16 | 2 bytes | Medium-High |
| bfloat16 | 16 | 2 bytes | Medium-High |
| int8 | 8 | 1 byte | Average |
| int4 (nf4) | 4 | 0.5 byte | Low-Medium |

-
### 2.2.1 QBT — Quantization Before Training
Here we change the dtype of the weights **before** training. For simple and fast projects, high precision can be useless and time consuming. So we have the option to do quantization before the training process, saving time and money.

'bfloat16' is generally preferred to 'float16' in this scenario, as it maintains the same dynamic range as 'float32' (same amount of bits in the exponent), being more numerically stable. 'float16' has higher fractional precision, but is more susceptible to overflow and underflow during training.

### 2.2.2 QDT — Quantization During Training (Mixed Precision)
Here, during training, we perform quantization dynamically. The most common technique is **Mixed Precision Training**.

The model maintains two types of weights at the same time: the **master weight**, kept in 'float32' to accumulate gradients with full accuracy, and the **operational weight**, converted to 'float16'/'bfloat16' in both forward and backward operations. At the end of each step, the gradients are converted back to 'float32' before updating the master weight, ensuring numerical stability without sacrificing efficiency.

To prevent small gradients from becoming zero in 'float16' (underflow), a **gradient scaler** is used that multiplies the loss by a scale factor before the backward, and reverses it after.


### 2.2.3 QAT — Quantization After Training (Post-Training Quantization)
Here we perform quantization **after** training, with the model already ready. The most widely used library for this is **bitsandbytes**, integrated with Hugging Face Transformers.

In **8-bit**, weights are compressed to 'int8', reducing memory by half with minimal loss of quality. In **4-bit**, the most common technique is **NF4 (NormalFloat4)**, a data type optimized for normally distributed weights, which is precisely the default for models trained with standard initialization.

It is also possible to apply **Double Quantization**, which quantizes the scale factors generated in the primary quantization themselves, saving about 0.4 additional bits per parameter.

This approach is the basis of **QLoRA**, which combines 4-bit quantization with LoRA adapters in 'bfloat16', enabling fine-tuning of large models on consumer GPUs like the RTX 3090/4090.

## 4.1 Cleaning, Transform and Define
NLP models are trained on text. Real text is a mess — HTML, emojis, URLs, broken encoding, duplicates, unbalanced data. If you shove garbage into the model, it learns garbage.


### 4.1.1 Noise removal
The raw text you collect often comes full of things that carry no linguistic meaning. As an example: 
- <p>Access: https://site.com!! 😂😂</p>

**What is the noise in the text?**
1. HTML Tags: < p>, < div>, < br>
2. URLs: https://site.com
3. Excessive punctuation: !!!, ...
4. Emojis and special characters: 😂, @#$%
5. Extra spaces, unnecessary line breaks
6. Numbers (depending on context)

**Why it's a problem?**

Because the model doesn't understand '😂' as "laughter" or < p> as "paragraph" — it sees them as tokens with no learned meaning.

Imagine you are training a spam classifier. Your vocabulary has words like "promotion", "free", "click here". If you don't clean it up, the model also learns tokens like < div>, !!!, https://t.co/xK92j — that vary infinitely and will never repeat usefully.
This causes two problems:
1. Inflated vocabulary — each different URL becomes a new token. Its vocabulary explodes with useless things, and the model spends capacity "memorizing" garbage.
2. Noise in learning — the model tries to find patterns in everything, including noise. This makes generalization worse.



### 4.1.2 Normalization (NLP)
Normalization is standardizing the text so that variations of the same thing are treated as equal.

    The problem without normalization:
    "Cat" != "cat" != "CAT"


The model treats these 3 as completely different words — 3 distinct tokens, 3 different embeddings. But they mean the same thing.

**In modern models (Transformers)**

That's not necessarily bad.
BERT and GPT already handle "Cat" and "cat" well — they learn from the context that they are the same thing. Their tokenizer (WordPiece, BPE) already handles this intelligently too.
So for Transformers, lowercase is not mandatory — and sometimes it hurts like I said in the table ("Apple" vs "apple").

**Where 3 different tokens is really bad:**

1. Classic models — TF-IDF, BoW, Naive Bayes. They have no context at all. "Cat" and "cat" become two completely separate features in the array. Vocabulary grows unnecessarily and you lose frequency count.

    without lowercase: "Cat"=1, "cat"=45, "CAT"=2 ← fragmented

    with lowercase: "cat"=48 ← unified

    Small datasets — if you have little data, fragmenting the same concept into multiple tokens makes each one appear fewer times, making learning difficult.

2. Small datasets — if you have little data, fragmenting the same concept into multiple tokens makes each one appear fewer times, making learning difficult.

> For transformers isn't necessary to do normalization!

### 4.1.3 Deduplication
Deduplication is removing repeated texts from the dataset.

**Why is it bad to have duplicates?**

The model sees the same text several times during training and gives it disproportionate weight — memorizes it instead of learning the general pattern.

    Imagine you have a dataset of 10,000 texts to train a classifier, and the text "Buy now with an unmissable discount" appears 500 times.
    During training the model sees this text repeated several times per epoch. It learns that that specific text is spam — not that patterns like "buy", "discount", "must-have" indicate spam.

**In practice this means:**

1. Overfitting — the model memorizes examples rather than generalizing. Works well in training, terrible in production with new texts.
2. Lying metrics — your accuracy in training is high but it's fake. The model is "gluing" to the repeated examples.
3. Bias in weights — that repeated text pulls the model weights in its direction. Other important standards are underrepresented.

It's the same problem as studying for the test by memorizing the answers instead of understanding the content — it works on the test, in a new situation you get stuck.

## 4.3 Chunkenization
### 4.3.1 Chunk: What is it and Why

Processing a text with 100 corns of tokens (or a complete book) is practically unfeasible today, as it would require a very extreme level of computation, the computational cost increases quadratically ($O(n^2)$). This means that if you double the text size (window), the GPU effort and memory expenditure do not double, they quadruple. A 100 million token text would require such a massive attention matrix that it wouldn't fit on every GPU in the world combined today. So we need to set a limit on how many tokens our model can read at a time, this assimilates a lot with human memory, which cannot remember everything, just a current context

So we need to define a boundary, a context window (our seq_len) to have a context and a 'memory'

### 4.3.2 Stratigies 

Since we can't have infinite windows, we use "tricks" to process long texts or hold long conversations:

1. **Chunkenization**: It is the "divide and conquer" strategy. We break the giant text into smaller pieces (chunks) that fit in seq_len. Example: In search systems (RAG), the model reads only the pieces of the book that are relevant to your question, rather than reading the entire book at once.

2. **Sliding Window**:Instead of discarding everything when the limit reaches 1024 tokens, the model "slides" the window. It keeps the last 1000 tokens and discards the oldest 24. This keeps the immediate context fresh every time.

3. **Summarization**: When memory is full, the system asks the model itself to summarize previous messages. We replace 500 history tokens with a 50-token summary. This "cleans" the table (VRAM) without completely losing the story of the conversation.

4. **KV Caching**:This is a hardware optimization strategy. To avoid reprocessing all words prior to each new token generated, the model saves the calculations of the passed attention layers (the $K$ and $V$ matrices) in a "cache". This saves a lot of GPU time.

5. **Sparse Attention**:Mathematical techniques (used in models like Gemini or Longformer) where the model decides not to look at all tokens against all tokens. It focuses only on nearest neighbors or important "global tokens", reducing the complexity from $O(n^2)$ to something closer to linear $O(n)$.

### 4.3.3 Chunk Overlap
Chunk Overlap (Chunk Overlay) is the "glue" we use when slicing text so that the template doesn't lose the meaning of the sentences that were cut.

Imagine you are cutting a rope into 1 meter pieces. If you cut exactly in the middle of a knot, the knot ceases to exist in both pieces. Overlap is like leaving 10cm to spare at each end so you can "tie" one piece of information to the other.

    Example without Overlap:

    Chunk 1: "The secret to training an AI model is the"
    Chunk 2: "descending gradient that adjusts the weights."

    The problem: The model reading Chunk 1 doesn't know what you're talking about ("is what?"). The model reading Chunk 2 receives a sentence that starts out of nowhere. Semantics broke down.

How it works (The example with Overlap)
    If we set a Chunk Size of 10 tokens and an Overlap of 3 tokens, the cut would look like this:

    Chunk 1: "The secret to training an AI model is the"

    Chunk 2: "AI is the gradient descent that adjusts the weights."

    Note that the expression "IA is the" appeared at the end of one and the beginning of the other. This creates a continuity of context

Tokenization it's the simple way to transform words into numbers, and have 2 steps

**Tokenizer Train:** The tokenizer training stage is responsible for building the vocabulary and assigning numerical IDs to tokens.

### 4.4.1 Words Level
* **Word-level (word-level) tokenization is the most direct and intuitive way to transform text into data for a neural network. In it, the fundamental unit of information is the entire word.**

If you have the phrase:

    "Vasco played well"
    the model sees three distinct entities: 
    " [Vasco], [played], [well]" 
    and each one distinct word will become a word
    {"Vasco": 1, "played": 2, "well": 3}.
> The OOV (Out-of-Vocabulary) Problem: If your model has learned "car" and "cars", but in the test it finds "cart", it will have no idea what that means. It will treat it as a special token called <UNK> (unknown).

* **OOV Solution: Special Tokens are "reserved words" that are not part of human language, but serve as technical signals to the neural network.**

1. [PAD] = Neural networks work with fixed-size matrices. If you have a 3-word sentence and a 10-word sentence, the network needs both to be the same size to enter the same "batch".
2. [UNK] = When your code finds a word that isn't in the dictionary (like slang or a typo), it replaces it with [UNK]. This prevents the code from breaking with a "word not found" error.
3. [SOS] or [CLS] = In generation models, the network needs a "trigger" to start writing.
4. [EOS] or [SEP] = It tells the network: "Stop generating text now, the sentence is over." Without this, a chatbot could write endlessly.

> Special tokens are necessary in ever level (character and subword too)

* **Advantages**
    - Direct Semantic Meaning: Each unit (token) already carries a complete concept. This makes it easier for the network to learn relationships between ideas (e.g., "Vasco" is close to "football") more quickly than if it had to learn how to put the letters together first.

    - Shorter Sequences: Since a 20-word sentence becomes exactly 20 tokens, the network's "attention window" is better leveraged. At the character level, this same sentence could have 120 tokens, which would require much more memory and processing.

    - Ease of Interpretation: It is much easier for you, as a developer, to debug what is entering the network. Seeing a tensioner [2, 45, 10] and knowing that it means "Vasco played well" helps a lot in monitoring training.

* **Disadvantages**
    - Vocabulary Explosion: If you want to process texts from the internet, you will have millions of unique words. This creates a gigantic Embedding array that can overflow your GPU memory.

    - The [UNK] Problem (OOV): This is the biggest weakness. If your model learned "run" and "we ran," but the test shows "ran," he can't take advantage of the root of the word. He will see something entirely new and mark it as "unknown", losing all the information.

    - Ignored Morphology: The network does not natively understand the structure of words. For her, "happy" and "unfortunately" are symbols as different as "ball" and "plane." She needs a lot of data to learn that these words have a common root.

    - Data Sparsity: Rare words that appear only once or twice in training are unlikely to have their vectors (embeddings) well trained, generating noise in the model.



### 4.4.2 Character Level
* **Character-level (character level) is the most "atomic" way of processing text. Instead of looking at words, the neural network breaks everything down into individual letters, numbers, and symbols.**

If you have:

    "Vasco"
    {"V": 1, "a": 2, "s": 3, "c": 4, "o": 5}
    The phrase will be the sequency of ID: [1, 2, 3, 4, 5].

* **Advantages**
    - Tiny Vocabulary: While word-level needs 50,000+ tokens, here you only need about 100 to 256 (ASCII letters, numbers and symbols). This saves a lot of memory in the embedding array.

    - OOV (Out-of-Vocabulary) Immunity: The "unknown word" error practically disappears. If the model knows the letters 'a' through 'z', it can process any word, even if it has never seen it before (such as "cart" or complex proper names).

    - Typing Errors: The model is more robust to typos (e.g. "Vascio"), as it sees that most of the letter sequence is still the same.

* **Disadvantages**
    - Very Long Sequences: A 10-word sentence can become 60 or 70 characters long. This requires significantly more processing power and network memory (especially in LSTMs or Transformers) to maintain context.

    - Semantic Difficulty: The network needs much more data and layers to understand that the union of casa represents a place where one lives. In word-level, this connection is immediate; here, it is learned.

### 4.4.3 SubWord Level
* **Subword Tokenization is the perfect "middle ground" and the technique that enabled the LLM revolution (GPT, Llama, Gemini). It was created precisely to solve the problems you saw in Word-level and Character-level. Where the root of the word is preserved, and its continuations become tokens too**

If you have the words:

    "ball" and "Vasco". As they appear a lot, they gain their own token. The root is the word itself.

But if you have the words:

    "Vascão", "Vasquinho", "Vascudo".The model notes that "Vasc" is the part that repeats the most (the root/radical).
    It holds "Vasc" as a token and creates separate tokens for the suffixes ("##ão", "m#inho", "m#udo").


#### Types of SubWords

* **1. BYPE** 

        This is the algorithm of GPT-2, GPT-3 and GPT-4. It focuses on frequency.
        How it works: It starts by treating each character as a token. It scans the text, finds the most repeating pair of tokens (e.g. e + s), and merges them into a new token (es). He repeats this thousands of times.

        The "Magic": Very common words end up becoming a token only quickly. Rare words are broken into pieces that appear elsewhere.

        Highlight (Byte-level): OpenAI uses a version that looks at the bytes of the text, which allows GPT to never see a word as "unknown" as it always manages to get to the byte level (0 to 255).

* **2. WordPiece**

        This is the algorithm of BERT and Google models. It focuses on probability.

        How it works: Much like BPE, but instead of just counting which pair appears most, it chooses the pair that, if merged, increases the model's probability of predicting the training data the most.

        The "##": You'll see this a lot on BERT. It uses the prefix ## to indicate that that piece is part of a larger word.

        Example: vasco + m#ista.

        Advantage: It is a bit stricter than BPE and tries to ensure that chunks (subwords) make maximum statistical sense.

* **3. Unigram and SentencePiece**

        Used by the T5, LLaMA and ALBERT. It focuses on loss (loss).

        Unigram: It starts with a huge vocabulary (all possible words and subwords) and removes the ones that least help the model explain the text, until the desired vocabulary size remains.

        SentencePiece: This library can run both BPE and Unigram. The big difference is that it treats whitespace as a symbol (usually a _).

        Why does it matter? In Word-level, space is what separates words. In SentencePiece, space is just another character. This allows the template to be "language-independent" (it works the same for Portuguese or Japanese, where there are no clear spaces).

* **Advantages**
    - Goodbye to [UNK] (Infinite Vocabulary): This is the biggest advantage. Since the model can break unknown words down to the letter level if necessary, it can represent any word. This prevents your chatbot from "crashing" or ignoring information when you use new slang or make a typo.

    - Memory Efficiency: With a relatively small vocabulary (between 32k and 128k tokens), you can cover multiple languages and programming codes. In Word-level, you would need millions of tokens to have the same coverage, which would require very expensive GPUs.

    - Morphological Intelligence (Roots and Affixes): The model learns that study- is the root of "study", "student" and "scholar". This allows knowledge to be shared between variations of the same word, making learning much faster and more logical.

    - Sequence Balance: Unlike Character-level, which generates gigantic and slow sequences, Subword keeps the sequence size manageable, allowing the model to process long texts efficiently.

* **Disadvantages**
    - Implementation Complexity: Unlike a simple.split(), you can't easily create a subword tokenizer manually. You need to "train" the tokenizer (BPE, WordPiece) on a large volume of text to learn which breaks are most statistically correct.

    - Processing Cost (Encoding/Decoding): There is an extra step. Before entering the network, the text needs to be encoded, and the network output needs to be decoded (put the pieces together). In very low latency systems, this can be a small bottleneck.

    - Non-Semantic Breaks: Since the criterion is statistical (frequency or probability), sometimes the algorithm breaks the word in a way that doesn't make grammatical sense to us.

            Example: Instead of Vasc + o, it can break into Va + sco if "sco" is a very common sequence in other words in the workout dataset.

    - Sensitivity to the Training Dataset: If the tokenizer was trained only on formal texts (books), it will have difficulty breaking internet slang efficiently, generating tokens that are too small (almost like the character level), which hinders performance.

## 4.6 Embedding
**Embedding it's the advanced way to transform words into numbers, while tokenization only allows each word or subword only 1 number. In embedd each word or subword as a sparse vector**

### 4.6.1 The Simple Tokenization Problem
In traditional tokenization, each word (or subword) is given only a unique **ID**.
* **Example:** "Cat" = 1, "Dog" = 2, "Refrigerator" = 3.
* **The Problem:** For the computer, the numbers 1 and 2 are unrelated. He doesn't know that a "Cat" is semantically closer to a "Dog" than to a "Refrigerator".
* **Sparsity:** If we use *One-Hot Encoding*, each word becomes a giant vector full of zeros with only one number '1'.

### 4.6.2 What are Embeddings?
Embedding turns this ID into a **dense vector** (a list of decimal numbers). 

#### The Matrix as a Trainable Parameter
A fundamental point is that the **Embedding Matrix is alive**:
* **Random Initialization:** At the beginning of the process, the array is filled with random numbers. The model doesn't know what the words mean.
* **Adjustment via Backpropagation:** During training, these numbers are treated as **parameters (weights)**. If the model misses a prediction, it adjusts the vector values to improve next time.
* **Convergence:** Over time, words that appear in similar contexts "travel" through mathematical space until they are close together. Embedding learning is the learning of concepts.

### 4.6.3 Vectors as Features (Features)
Each dimension of the vector represents an abstract "feature". Imagine that the AI model creates invisible columns to define the word:

| Word | Feature: Animal | Feature: Domestic | Feature: Royalty |
| :- | :---: | :---: | :---: |
| **Dog** | 0.99 | 0.95 | 0.02 |
| **Cat** | 0.98 | 0.91 | 0.05 |
| **King** | 0.10 | 0.02 | 0.97 |

> **Note:** Values are decimal between -1 and 1 (or 0 and 1). Words with similar numerical "profiles" are treated as similar concepts by AI.


### 4.6.4 The Magic of Proximity and Vector Algebra
Since words are now mathematical coordinates, we can calculate the distance between them and even perform logical operations:

#### A. Cosine Similarity
AI measures the angle between two vectors. If the angle is small, the words are synonymous or in the same context.

#### B. Mathematical Analogies
The model can capture gender, tense, and capital relationships of countries through sums and subtractions:
$$Vector(\text{King}) - Vector(\text{Man}) + Vector(\text{Woman}) \approx Vector(\text{Queen})$$
